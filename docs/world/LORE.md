# The RinkGM Lore Bible

Everything here is fictional. Any resemblance to a real league, brand, rink or
person with a moustache is the kind of resemblance you get when two things
grew up in the same cold climate.

The valley is where you play. The Grand Northern Hockey League is where
everybody in the valley pretends they could have played. It sits far above
Millbrook, Harlow Falls, Kessler, Port Ainsley and Duncannon, on television
on Saturday nights, on the calendar at the hardware store, and on the faded
banner above the snack bar in the Millbrook Community Arena.

---

## 1. The Grand Northern Hockey League

### The founding: the Blizzard Charter of 1911

In the second week of January 1911, eight club owners boarded the overnight
train to Kingsport for what was meant to be a one-evening meeting about
"standardising the size of the puck, which in some towns is a disgrace."
Somewhere past the Ottershaw junction the train ran into the worst blizzard
in forty years and stopped dead in a snowdrift beside the Hotel Lindell, a
railway hotel with nineteen rooms, one piano and a dining room that ran out
of everything but potatoes on the second day.

They were there for three days. By the end of the first, they had agreed on
the puck (one inch thick, three across, frozen before play "where practical").
By the end of the second, nobody was speaking to the owner of the Port Hollis
club, who had won eleven dollars from everyone at cards. By the end of the
third, they had written the constitution of a new league on the back of the
hotel's dinner menu, which still survives in a glass case in the league
offices. Under "Soup of the Day" it reads, in pencil: *Article 1. There shall
be a league, and it shall be Grand, and it shall be Northern.*

Nobody can remember who suggested "Grand." Three families claim it.

The eight founding clubs, the Snowbound Eight, were:

- Kingsport Monarchs
- Bramford Iron
- Ste-Colette Coureurs
- Northgate Foresters
- Marrow Bay Gulls
- Fairhaven Squalls
- Port Hollis Pioneers (folded 1934)
- Ottershaw Millers (folded 1944)

The first season began on 2 December 1911 on outdoor ice in Kingsport. The
Monarchs beat the Iron 4-3 in a game delayed twice for snow shovelling and
once because the puck went into the river.

### The Puckworth Cup

Sir Reginald Puckworth was a lumber baron, a patron of the arts and, by his
own admission, a man who had never watched a hockey game all the way through.
In 1912 he ordered a large silver punch bowl from a London silversmith as a
prize for the Kingsport Curling Club. The curling club, in a dispute about
dues, declined it. Sir Reginald, unwilling to send it back and pay the
shipping twice, gave it to the new hockey league "on the condition that it
be awarded each year to the finest hockey club in the Dominion, and that
nobody drink out of it."

Everybody has drunk out of it.

The deed of gift, with its loose wording about "the finest hockey club,"
caused trouble exactly once, during the Lost Winter of 2005 (see below). The
Cup has since grown a tall barrel of engraved bands underneath the original
bowl, and the oldest bands are retired to the league's Hall of Honour every
thirteen years so the thing can still fit through a door. It has been
dropped in Marrow Bay harbour (1952), left on a curb in Bramford (1971),
used as a planter for geraniums by a Coureurs defenceman's mother (1983) and
taken through a car wash, with permission (2011). It has a dent on the left
handle that the league describes as "character."

Each player on the winning team gets one day with the Cup in the summer. The
Cup has visited fishing camps, christenings, a tractor pull and, in 1994,
the Millbrook Community Arena.

### Timeline of eras

**The Frontier Era (1911-1928).** Seven-man hockey with a rover, outdoor ice,
no forward passing, and a rule forbidding the goaltender from leaving his
feet. Goalies who fell down were fined two dollars. Goalies who fell down
*cleverly* were fined three. The Ste-Colette Coureurs and their ghostly
centre Aurele Fontaine owned the period. The 1919 Cup was not awarded at all
because of the Great Thaw, a warm February that turned every rink in the
league into a pond and the final into "a regrettable paddling contest."

**The Wool Era (1929-1945).** Artificial ice arrived, the rover was
abolished, and in 1929 the league legalised the forward pass inside each zone.
Newspapers called it "the death of hockey," "an invitation to laziness" and,
in one Bramford editorial, "continental." Scoring doubled in a season.
Within three years everyone pretended they had always been for it. The Great
Depression took the Port Hollis Pioneers in 1934, and the Ottershaw Millers
went in 1944 when their rink was requisitioned to store army boots.

**The Six Era (1946-1966).** Six clubs, seventy games, a lot of train travel,
and a dynasty in Kingsport. Walter Brzezinski stood in the Monarchs net with
no mask and Gentleman Jim Harrowgate scored without ever seeming to take a
breath or a penalty. Black-and-white television arrived and the league learned
that a white puck on white ice was a problem, and that a black puck on black
and white television was mostly a rumour.

**The Great Expansion (1967-1979).** The league added the Granite City
Stonecutters in 1967. In 1972 it absorbed its upstart rival, the Trans-Prairie
Hockey Association, after a three-year bidding war that had driven the price
of a decent defenceman up to a new car and a house. Only one Trans-Prairie
club survived the merger: the Redstone Rockets, who arrived with chrome
helmets and an attitude. The late seventies were the Lumber Years, when every
team carried at least one player whose job description was "deterrent." Moose
Kowalczuk set the penalty minutes record that nobody, including the league,
wants broken.

**The Run-and-Gun Era (1980-1996).** Skating got faster, goalies got bigger,
and then forwards got so good that the goalies' bigness did not help. Teddy
Varga and the valley's own Ronnie Haverstock rewrote the scoring books.
In 1993 the league made its first "warm-weather" expansion, to Sunvale, a
desert city where the arena's ice plant uses more power than the airport.

**The Clutch Years (1997-2004).** Scores collapsed. Teams discovered they
could hook, hold and lean on any opponent for sixty minutes and only be
called for it in the final thirty seconds of a tied playoff game. The Lake
Ostrander Loons joined in 2001 and were immediately abbreviated LOL by the
schedule printers, which the league still tries to fix by calling them OST.

**The Lost Winter (2004-2005).** The lockout. See below.

**The Modern Era (2005-present).** Out of the lockout came a salary cap,
the shootout, crackdowns on obstruction, a trapezoid behind each net, and
seventeen third jerseys. Analytics departments arrived and started telling
coaches things they did not want to hear. In 2018 the league expanded to
Silverton, a casino town, whose Specters won the Cup in their second season
and have been unbearable about it ever since.

### The Lost Winter

The 2004-05 lockout is the only time in its history the GNHL cancelled a full
season. The dispute began over a salary cap and ended over a salary cap, but
for forty-one days in the middle it was about sandwiches. Owners and players
had agreed to negotiate in the Hotel Lindell, the same railway hotel where the
league was founded, for sentimental reasons. The hotel's catering contract
covered "light lunch." The players' association insisted this meant hot
food. The owners insisted it did not. Both sides brought their own lunches
for a month and a half, and the newspapers called it the Sandwich Clause.

With no season, the question arose of who would get the Puckworth Cup.
Sir Reginald's deed said it went to "the finest hockey club in the Dominion,"
and said nothing about it having to be a professional one. A Beer League team
from Millbrook, the Millbrook Mallards, wrote to the Cup trustees in neat
handwriting asking to be considered. The letter was signed by eleven
players, two of whom had day jobs as a dentist and a snowplough driver.

The trustees did not award them the Cup. They did, however, ship it to
Millbrook for one night in March 2005, where it sat on a folding table at
centre ice of the Millbrook Community Arena while about four hundred people
queued in the cold to have their picture taken with it. The photo of the
Mallards around the Cup still hangs in the lobby. The engraving for 2005 on
the Cup itself reads *SEASON NOT PLAYED*, and below it, very small, in letters
the engraver swore were his own idea: *MILLBROOK ASKED.*

### Traditions

- **Fish on the ice.** Marrow Bay fans throw a frozen herring onto the ice
  after the first Gulls goal of a playoff run. The arena crew keeps a
  dedicated herring shovel.
- **Mittens in Fairhaven.** When a Squalls player scores a hat trick, the
  crowd throws mittens instead of hats, because it is very cold in Fairhaven
  and nobody wants to give up a hat. The mittens go to the children's
  hospital. The Canadian Mitten Company sponsors this and pretends not to.
- **The playoff beard.** No player on a team still in the playoffs may shave.
  Rookies have been seen with eyeliner moustaches out of solidarity.
- **The Kingsport organ.** The Monarchs still have a live organist. The
  current one, Mrs Eunice Tolland, has held the post since 1981 and plays a
  passive-aggressive dirge whenever the referee misses a call.
- **The handshake line.** After every playoff series both teams shake hands,
  including players who were trying to remove each other's heads an hour
  earlier. It is the most sincere thing in hockey and nobody can explain it.
- **Don't touch the Platter.** The semifinal trophy, the Hollingsworth
  Platter, is considered bad luck to touch. Captains accept it with a nod,
  pose next to it like it's a stranger's dog, and leave.
- **Saturday Night Hockey.** The league's flagship broadcast, on air since the
  radio days, intermission segment and all. Its long-running intermission
  feature, *Moose on the Loose*, is hosted by Moose Kowalczuk, who wears a
  different plaid jacket every week and has never repeated one.

**The affiliate in the valley.** The Duncannon Dukes, the Minor League Pro
club that plays in Duncannon's modern 5,000-seat arena, are the Kingsport
Monarchs' affiliate. The Monarchs like the name. Dukes are one step below
monarchs, which is exactly where Kingsport thinks everyone should be. The
Dukes' general manager keeps a red rotary phone on his desk that rings only
when Kingsport wants a player. It is the "phone call from the show," and in
Duncannon nobody is allowed to touch it.

### Rivalries and controversies

**The Hundred Years' Grudge (Kingsport vs Ste-Colette).** It began at the
Hotel Lindell in 1911, when the Kingsport owner was given the bigger room.
It continued through 33 playoff meetings, including the 1955 Final, decided
in the fourth overtime by a goal that went in off a Coureurs player's skate,
a Monarchs player's stick and, according to Ste-Colette, a Kingsport
referee's ambition. The two clubs' fans are still not seated in adjoining
sections, and the Kingsport organist plays the Coureurs onto the ice with a
funeral march.

**The Highway 9 War (Bramford vs Northgate).** The two towns are ninety
minutes apart on the same two-lane highway, which both teams' buses used to
share, which was the problem. The low point came on 11 March 1977, when a
second-period scrap emptied both benches, then both penalty boxes, then the
press box, and finally rolled out through the loading doors into the parking
lot, where it ended only because it was minus thirty-one and everybody had
taken their gloves off. The league fined both teams the same amount, to the
dollar, so neither could claim it had won.

**The Phantom Goal (1971 Final, Game 7).** Granite City's Lyle Pettibone
shot from the blue line in the final minute. The goal judge's light went on.
The referee said the puck never crossed the line. The puck was never found.
Stonecutters fans believe it went through a hole in the back of the net and
into the snow outside. Bramford, who won the Cup instead, believe the light
was "a fault in the wiring, and in Granite City's character." In 2011 a
construction crew found a puck in the arena's old foundation and the
argument started again from the beginning.

**The Trade (1989).** Teddy Varga, the best player in the world, was traded
from Granite City to Sunvale for two players, three draft picks, and a sum of
money that was announced as "undisclosed" and printed by every newspaper the
next morning. Granite City fans burned replica Varga jerseys on the courthouse
steps, then queued to buy new Sunvale ones so they could keep watching him.
It made hockey work in the desert and it broke the heart of a city that is
literally made of rock.

**The Mask Debate (1958).** When Monarchs goaltender Walter Brzezinski was
asked whether goalies should wear masks, he said, "If God wanted goalies to
have faces, He wouldn't have invented the puck." The league took him at his
word for another decade. It is now considered one of the worst safety
decisions in sporting history, and Brzezinski, who had about four hundred
stitches by retirement, agreed with that in his old age.

### Rule-change fights, in brief

- **1929, the forward pass.** "The end of hockey." It wasn't.
- **1958, the mask.** "Cowardice." It wasn't.
- **1979, the helmet rule.** Helmets were mandatory for new players only,
  so veterans who had never worn one played helmetless into the 1990s. Moose
  Kowalczuk's last helmetless game, in 1987, got a standing ovation and a
  concussion.
- **1999, the Toe Goal.** A Cup-winning goal was scored with a skater's toe
  barely in the crease. The crease rule was abolished that summer, and the
  losing city still sells "IT WAS IN THE CREASE" T-shirts.
- **2005, the shootout.** Purists hated it. Children loved it. Goalies are
  still undecided.
- **2006, the trapezoid.** A painted area behind each net where goalies may
  play the puck. Its shape was chosen, the league insists, by an engineer and
  not by the person who drew the logo.

## 2. Legends and Villains

Skater lines read GP, G, A, PTS, PIM. Goalie lines read GP, W, GAA, SV%.
Statistics before 1955 were reconstructed by the league's historians from
newspaper box scores, which is why some of them end in a footnote and an
argument. The GNHL season is 78 games today; it was 20 to 30 in the
Frontier Era and 70 in the Six Era.

### Aurele "The Ghost" Fontaine
**C, 1912-1928. Ste-Colette Coureurs.**
GP 312, G 241, A 98, PTS 339, PIM 140. **Puckworth Cups: 4.**
*Reputation: the first superstar; the fastest skater of the outdoor age.*

A farm boy from the hills above Ste-Colette, Fontaine was so pale and so
quiet that opponents said they never saw him until he was already past them.
He scored seven goals in one game in 1916, on a night so foggy that the
Kingsport papers claimed he did not exist and the Coureurs had simply been
sending pucks into the net with a rope. He was the first player whose
picture appeared on a cereal box, and the cereal company misspelled his name,
which made the cards collectable. He retired to run a hardware store and
sharpened local children's skates for free until he was ninety.

### Hector "Old Lumber" MacInnes
**D, 1924-1943. Bramford Iron.**
GP 701, G 88, A 203, PTS 291, PIM 1,022. **Puckworth Cups: 3.**
*Reputation: the immovable defenceman; one stick, one career (allegedly).*

MacInnes claimed to have played his entire nineteen-year career with a single
Shorewood ash stick, retaped more than four hundred times. Shorewood built a
whole advertising campaign around it, and it is still the story old-timers
tell when someone in the dressing room breaks a composite stick. After his
death, his son admitted that Hector had owned "about thirty of them," all
the same model, and simply never let anyone see him change. Shorewood has
never acknowledged this. MacInnes was the first defenceman to deliberately
block shots, which he did with his shins, his chest and, once, his face.

### Walter "The Wall" Brzezinski
**G, 1946-1966. Kingsport Monarchs.**
GP 962, W 487, GAA 2.31, SV% .918 (reconstructed). **Puckworth Cups: 7.**
*Reputation: the greatest goalie of the Six Era; famously, foolishly maskless.*

Brzezinski played twenty seasons without a mask and spent his summers having
his face put back together. He won seven Cups, kept a jar of his own teeth
on the dressing room shelf as a warning to rookies, and treated every goal
against him as a personal insult from the shooter's family. His quote about
masks (see the Mask Debate) is one of the most repeated lines in the sport.
In retirement he became a gentle, cardigan-wearing goalie coach who made every
child he taught wear two masks, "one for you and one for me."

### "Gentleman" Jim Harrowgate
**RW, 1951-1970. Kingsport Monarchs.**
GP 1,180, G 512, A 601, PTS 1,113, PIM 34. **Puckworth Cups: 6.**
*Reputation: the cleanest great player who ever lived; somehow still hated in Ste-Colette.*

Harrowgate went nine full seasons without taking a single penalty and
apologised to referees when he was offside. He wore a tie to every game and
a tie under his sweater during it. The league's sportsmanship award is named
after him, and every player who wins it is legally required to look a little
embarrassed. In Ste-Colette he is remembered differently: he scored the
fourth-overtime goal in the 1955 Final, off three things, and has been
publicly forgiven by the Coureurs exactly once, by accident, in a eulogy.

### Denis "Le Docteur" Bellerose
**LW, 1968-1984. Ste-Colette Coureurs.**
GP 1,050, G 560, A 588, PTS 1,148, PIM 290. **Puckworth Cups: 5.**
*Reputation: the most stylish player of the seventies; the hair, the shot, the cigarettes (later regretted).*

Bellerose was called The Doctor because he once told a reporter he was
"operating" on Kingsport's defence, and because he wore a long camel coat to
the rink like a man about to make a house call. He had the best wrist shot of
his generation and the best hair of any generation. He skated with no helmet
until the end, let his hair stream behind him like a flag, and became the
first hockey player to endorse a shampoo. He now owns three restaurants and
a racehorse called Overtime, which has never won.

### Moose Kowalczuk
**D / enforcer, 1974-1987. Bramford Iron, Northgate Foresters, Redstone Rockets.**
GP 812, G 41, A 77, PTS 118, PIM 3,644. **Puckworth Cups: 1.**
*Reputation: the Mayor of the Penalty Box; now the most beloved man on television.*

The all-time penalty minutes leader, by a distance the league describes as
"an era." Kowalczuk was six foot five, soft-spoken and a voracious reader,
and spent so much time in the penalty box that he kept a paperback in it
during home games. He fought every enforcer of his generation, lost almost
none and never hit anyone from behind. He fought on both sides of the Highway
9 War, and was traded mid-season in 1980 to the very team he had fought the
night before; he shook hands with everyone he'd punched before practice.
Today he hosts *Moose on the Loose* on Saturday Night Hockey, wears loud
plaid, cries at retirements and is the face of Brokenbone Mutual Insurance
("Moose knows").

### Ronnie "The Milkman" Haverstock
**C, 1979-1996. Granite City Stonecutters, Kingsport Monarchs.**
GP 1,244, G 503, A 822, PTS 1,325, PIM 410. **Puckworth Cups: 2.**
*Reputation: the valley's own; the late bloomer from Millbrook who made the show.*

Ronnie Haverstock was born in Millbrook and drove the milk truck for the
Millbrook Dairy until he was twenty-two, playing Beer League on Tuesday nights
at the Millbrook Community Arena. A Granite City scout, stuck in the valley
by a snowstorm (this is a recurring theme in GNHL history), wandered into the
rink to get warm and watched Haverstock score five goals on the arena's
notoriously crooked ice. Haverstock had learned to use a dead spot in the
boards behind the Millbrook net, where the puck stopped instead of bouncing,
to set up passes nobody saw coming. In the GNHL it became the "Millbrook
bank," a soft chip off the end boards that only he could make work.

He played in the GNHL for seventeen seasons, won two Cups late in his career
with Kingsport, and brought the Cup home to Millbrook in the summer of 1994,
where it sat on the counter of the dairy for an afternoon. His number 14
hangs in the rafters of the Millbrook Community Arena, from a hook that has
been replaced three times, beside a slightly faded photo of him in his milk
delivery uniform. He still lives on the edge of town and plays in the
Thursday old-timers' game, where he passes the puck off the same dead spot,
which the rink has never fixed out of respect.

### Teddy "Wonderboy" Varga
**C, 1982-2001. Granite City Stonecutters, Sunvale Scorpions.**
GP 1,460, G 742, A 1,311, PTS 2,053, PIM 512. **Puckworth Cups: 4.**
*Reputation: the best there ever was, and also the subject of The Trade.*

Varga was a skinny kid who looked like he'd borrowed his equipment from an
older brother. He saw the ice like it was a map he'd drawn himself. He won
two Cups in Granite City, was traded to Sunvale in 1989 in the deal that broke
a city, and won two more in the desert, where his arrival tripled the number
of public rinks in the region. He holds most of the league's scoring records,
including some that look like typing errors. He played with a Kodiak stick
all his career and once said Grafton skates "made his feet feel judged."

### Barry "Suitcase" Pomeroy
**D, 1985-2004. Nine GNHL clubs, plus seven stints with the Duncannon Dukes.**
GP 1,022, G 47, A 211, PTS 258, PIM 1,188. **Puckworth Cups: 1.**
*Reputation: the most beloved journeyman in hockey; never the best, always welcome.*

Pomeroy played for nine of the league's eleven clubs (he never played for
Silverton, which did not exist, or Lake Ostrander, which he says he "just
never got round to"). He was traded at three deadlines in a row, once at the
airport. He was sent down to the Duncannon Dukes seven times and called back
up seven times, and the Duncannon fans gave him a standing ovation every time
he arrived and every time he left. He kept a packed suitcase by the front
door for nineteen years. He won his only Cup in his final game, in 2004 with
Fairhaven, and was carried around the ice by teammates half his age. His
suitcase is in the Hall of Honour. It still has a Duncannon luggage tag on it.

### Ricky "Slick" Delorme
**RW, 1990-1998. Sunvale Scorpions, Redstone Rockets.**
GP 540, G 201, A 189, PTS 390, PIM 602. **Puckworth Cups: 0.**
*Reputation: the Wrong Twin scandal; the most famous suspension in league history.*

Delorme was a good winger with excellent teeth and an identical twin, Donnie,
who was a better hockey player and did not want to be one. In February 1996
Ricky wanted to attend a celebrity golf tournament in the middle of the
season. Donnie played eleven games in his place. Nobody noticed, partly
because they had the same haircut and partly because Donnie played *better*,
scoring six goals in eleven games while being much nicer to the media. He was
caught when he signed an autograph with his own name. Ricky was suspended for
a season. Donnie was offered a contract by three teams and turned them all
down to go back to running a bakery. The six goals remain in Ricky's career
totals with an asterisk, and the league's rulebook now contains Rule 11.4,
"Players shall be the player they say they are."

### Patrice "The Octopus" Gendron
**G, 1993-2012. Ste-Colette Coureurs, Marrow Bay Gulls.**
GP 1,012, W 541, GAA 2.41, SV% .914. **Puckworth Cups: 3.**
*Reputation: the butterfly master; arms everywhere, a calm and deeply strange man.*

Gendron dropped to his knees and spread his pads like nobody had before, and
seemed to have more arms than a goalie should. He talked to his goalposts,
thanked them after wins and refused to speak to them after losses. He wore
Ironside pads for his whole career and an unchanging mask painted with a
purple octopus, which became the bestselling goalie mask design in the
league's history. He moved to Marrow Bay late in his career, where fans
started throwing a frozen octopus with their herring, and the arena crew
had to buy a second shovel.

### Viktor "The Ice Weasel" Sokolov
**C / agitator, 2001-2016. Redstone Rockets, Northgate Foresters, Lake Ostrander Loons.**
GP 1,003, G 278, A 344, PTS 622, PIM 1,490. **Puckworth Cups: 1.**
*Reputation: the most hated player of his generation; also extremely good, which made it worse.*

Sokolov never won a fight and never needed to. He chirped, slashed, flopped,
tapped goalies' pads after the whistle, and once squirted an opposing
goalie's own water bottle at him. The league responded with the "Sokolov
Rule" (no touching the other team's water bottles), the "Second Sokolov Rule"
(no touching the other team's bench) and a memo, never made public, known
only as "The Third One." He was also a gifted centre who scored in big games
and won a Cup with Northgate in 2009, where fans who had booed him for six
years carried him through the streets. He is now a children's television
presenter, and is very good at it.

### Morris "Emergency Mo" Teague
**G, 2009. Fairhaven Squalls.**
GP 3, W 3, GAA 1.67, SV% .944. **Puckworth Cups: 1.**
*Reputation: the accountant who won the Cup.*

Every GNHL team must have an emergency backup goalie available at home games.
In the 2009 Final, Fairhaven's was Morris Teague, 41, the club's payroll
accountant, who had played Beer League A in Harlow Falls on Wednesdays. When
both Squalls goalies were injured in Game 4, Teague was dressed, put in net
and asked if he'd like to say a few words. He said "I'll do my best," stopped
fifty of fifty-three shots across three games and won the Cup. He went back
to work the following Monday, processed the playoff bonuses, including his
own, and has never played a professional game since. His goalie gear,
bought from Honest Hockey Co., is in the Hall of Honour with the price
stickers still on.

### Theo "The Metronome" Lindgren-Baptiste
**C, 2014-present. Marrow Bay Gulls.**
GP 862, G 401, A 611, PTS 1,012, PIM 88. **Puckworth Cups: 2.**
*Reputation: the modern superstar; the analytics department's favourite person on earth.*

The best player in the league today is a polite, unflashy centre who has
never missed a game, never takes a bad shift, and whose numbers are so
consistent that the Marrow Bay data team uses them to calibrate their
software. He is famous for doing nothing interesting off the ice: he reads
about bridges, eats the same breakfast every day and once described a
Puckworth Cup win as "a really good week." He has a Warlock stick deal and
visibly does not understand the advertisements, which feature him growling
in a thunderstorm.

## 3. The Gear Brands

Every piece of gear in the world comes from one of these companies, and every
player in the valley has an opinion about all of them.

### Kodiak
**Heritage premium skates and sticks.** Founded 1898.
*Slogan: "Built Like Weather."*

Josiah Brandt was a blacksmith in the timber town of Bearpaw Lake who made
skate blades for loggers. In 1898 a brown bear broke into his storehouse and
ate his entire first shipment of leather boots, which he took as a sign that
he was making boots worth eating. He renamed the company after the bear, and
the bear became the logo, a kind of confession. Kodiak is the brand your dad
and his dad wore: solid, a bit heavy, built to last fifteen years and look
better for it. Its catalogues still use the word "gentlemen."

**Personality:** Grandfatherly, proud, slow to change, quietly excellent.
**Logo:** A brown bear's head inside a shield with "EST. 1898" across the bottom. Bear brown #5B3A1E, parchment #E8DCC0, Kodiak red #A3262A.

**Product lines**
- *Grizzly Pro* skates: the flagship boot, stiff, heavy, legendary.
- *Heritage 1898* skates: retro leather look, modern inside; dads buy them for themselves.
- *Cub* skates: youth line, with the bear smiling.
- *Timberline* sticks: classic composite, mid kick, 75 / 85 / 95 flex.
- *Summit* sticks: the top model, low kick, 70 / 85 / 100 flex.
- *Old Growth* gloves: thick leather palms that take a season to break in.

### CMC, the Canadian Mitten Company
**Helmets, gloves, shoulder pads, shin guards.** Founded 1921.
*Slogan: "We've Always Made Gloves."*

The Canadian Mitten Company made mittens. Very good mittens. In 1926 a
Northgate defenceman complained that his hockey gloves were "about as warm
as a handshake," so CMC sold him a pair of their mittens with a hole cut
for the thumb and a strip of leather down the back. He won the Cup in them.
CMC has made hockey gloves ever since and has spent ninety years insisting
it was always a glove company, that "mitten" is a legacy word in the name,
that the official corporate abbreviation stands for nothing in particular,
and that the woollen cuff on every CMC glove is "a performance feature."
The company still makes mittens, at a small factory round the back, and
won't discuss it.

**Personality:** Dependable, a little defensive, secretly cosy.
**Logo:** A rounded crest the company calls "the Shield." It is a mitten. Mitten red #C8102E, white #FFFFFF, wool grey #8A8D8F.

**Product lines**
- *Tundra* helmets: the classic league helmet, available in "Shield Red."
- *Frostline* gloves: the pro glove, with the woollen cuff they won't explain.
- *Bulwark* shin guards and elbow pads.
- *Hearthguard* shoulder pads: "Like a warm hug that stops a slapshot."
- *Not Mittens* youth gloves, a name decided by a committee that has since been dissolved.

### Warlock Hockey
**Composite sticks, plus aggressive skates and gloves.** Founded 2003.
*Slogan: "Unleash the Dark."*

Warlock was started by two former marketing executives from an energy drink
company who had never played hockey but understood that sixteen-year-olds
like the colour black. Their first ad featured a stick being forged in a
volcano, which is not how composite sticks are made. Their second featured a
stick being forged in a different volcano. Warlock sticks are, irritatingly,
very good, which means the dressing rooms of the world are full of grown men
holding equipment called the *Hexblade* and pretending it's normal.

**Personality:** Loud, dark, extremely online, and better than it has any right to be.
**Logo:** A jagged five-pointed star in a circle that the company insists is "a puck viewed from an aggressive angle." Void black #121212, curse purple #6A1B9A, acid green #9BE22D.

**Product lines**
- *Hexblade* sticks: the bestseller, low kick, 65 / 75 / 85 / 95 / 102 flex.
- *Grimoire* sticks: the "elite" model, with a spell printed along the shaft (it's the warranty terms).
- *Curse* sticks: entry level, 55 / 65 / 75 flex, "for apprentices."
- *Ritual* skates: black, stiff, mostly marketing, oddly comfortable.
- *Summoner* gloves: black with purple palms and a green skull on the cuff.

### Honest Hockey Co.
**Value gear, top to bottom.** Founded 1988.
*Slogan: "It's Fine. It'll Do the Job."*

Founded in Harlow Falls by Dorothy Pruitt, a retired schoolteacher who was
tired of paying four hundred dollars for her grandson's skates and watching
him outgrow them by Easter. Honest Hockey makes plain, decent gear at a fair
price, and its advertising is famous for saying exactly what the product is.
Its boxes read "These skates are not the best skates. They are good skates.
They will be fine." The company has won more trust than any marketing
department in hockey, mostly by refusing to have one. It is the gear of
every beer league in the valley, and Emergency Mo Teague won the Cup in it.

**Personality:** Candid, frugal, kind, allergic to adjectives.
**Logo:** The words HONEST HOCKEY CO. in plain capitals inside a plain rectangle. Cardboard tan #C9A66B, ink navy #1D2B4F, off-white #F5F1E6.

**Product lines**
- *The Adequate* skate: "Comfortable enough. Sharpen it."
- *The Stick*: one model, two flexes, 70 and 85, labelled "Bendier" and "Less Bendy."
- *The Glove*: "It is a glove. It protects your hand."
- *The Pads*: shin and elbow; "Worn by people who don't want to get hurt."
- *The Better One*: a slightly better skate, "for if you're keen."

### Shorewood
**Wooden sticks.** Founded 1894, the oldest brand in the game.
*Slogan: "Real Wood. Real Hockey. Real Splinters."*

Shorewood began as a sawmill on the shore of Lake Ostrander that made oars,
axe handles and, when the foreman got bored, hockey sticks. The sticks
outsold the oars by 1900. Shorewood still steam-bends its blades by hand and
still believes composite sticks are a passing fad, a view it has held
firmly since 1990. Old-timers swear by them. They say the puck "feels like
something" off wood, that a Shorewood will last if you look after it, and
that Old Lumber MacInnes used one for his whole career (see above, and don't
mention the other twenty-nine).

**Personality:** Stubborn, nostalgic, smells like a woodshop, extremely proud of the MacInnes story.
**Logo:** A crossed pair of wooden sticks over a lake-and-pine woodcut in an oval. Ash blonde #D9B98A, varnish brown #7B4A26, lake green #2F5D50.

**Product lines**
- *Classic 5030*: the ash stick your grandfather used, 85 flex ("Honest").
- *Old Hickory*: heavier, stiffer, 100 flex ("Stubborn").
- *Lumberman's Special*: a fibreglass-wrapped wood stick, 75 flex ("Soft"), the only compromise the company has ever made.
- *Goalie Paddle*: a wooden goalie stick that weighs as much as a small dog.
- *The MacInnes*: a signature replica, sold with a roll of tape and a wink.

### Grafton Blades
**Skates, blade holders and steel.** Founded 1962.
*Slogan: "You Wouldn't Understand."*

The Grafton family made surgical instruments and watch springs before
Leopold Grafton decided in 1962 that skate steel was "being made by people
with no respect for the edge." Grafton skates are precise, beautiful,
heat-moulded, absurdly expensive, and come in a wooden presentation box with
a cleaning cloth and a small card reminding you not to walk on concrete in
them. Grafton representatives refer to the hollow of a sharpening in
microns, to customers as "clients," and to Kodiak as "a heritage company,"
which they mean as an insult. The skates are, unfortunately, wonderful.

**Personality:** Precise, snobbish, quietly brilliant, wears a scarf indoors.
**Logo:** A thin silver blade underlining the word GRAFTON in narrow capitals. Platinum #D6D9DC, graphite #2E3133, sapphire #2457A6.

**Product lines**
- *Apex Sabre* skates: the pro boot; fitting is by appointment.
- *Meridian* skates: the "entry level," priced like a used car.
- *Edge 9* steel: replacement runners, sold in a felt sleeve.
- *Atelier* holders: "Designed. Not made."
- *Aperture* custom service: they scan your feet, then sigh.

### Ironside Goalie Co.
**Goalie pads, blockers, catchers, masks.** Founded 1958.
*Slogan: "Stand in Front of Things."*

When her husband, a Bramford Iron backup goalie, came home with a broken
collarbone in 1958, Margaret Ironside took apart the living room sofa and
sewed him a set of pads that were twice as thick as anything on the market.
He was the best-protected goalie in the league for the rest of his career,
and the family never got a new sofa. Ironside is the goalie brand: serious,
padded, and run by people who understand that goaltenders are strange and
should be supported anyway.

**Personality:** Protective, practical, a bit like a good aunt.
**Logo:** A stylised goalie pad standing upright like a castle tower. Castle grey #5E6A71, safety orange #F28C28, white #FFFFFF.

**Product lines**
- *Bastion* leg pads: the pro pad; Patrice Gendron's choice.
- *Rampart* blockers.
- *Keep* catch gloves, with a deep pocket "for holding on."
- *Portcullis* masks, cage and fibreglass shell.
- *Sofa Classic* retro pads: tan leather, stitched like 1958.

### Gripley's Tape
**Stick tape, shin tape and wax.** Founded 1949.
*Slogan: "Holds Sticks Together. Also Mufflers, Marriages and Most Hockey Bags."*

Arnold Gripley sold electrical tape door to door in Kessler until a mill-town
team bought his entire stock to tape their sticks. Gripley's has made hockey
tape ever since and still sells more of it to people who are not playing
hockey than to people who are. Every dressing room in the valley has a pile
of half-used Gripley's rolls in a bucket, and every rink manager has fixed
something structural with one.

**Personality:** Handy, cheerful, small-town, used for everything.
**Logo:** A tape roll seen side on, drawn as a thick black ring with a white centre. Tape black #1A1A1A, cloth white #F4F4F0, Gripley yellow #F2C230.

**Product lines**
- *Gripley Cloth*: the classic black and white stick tape.
- *Gripley Clear*: shin pad tape, "for socks that won't stay up."
- *Gripley Wax*: for blades; smells like a candle shop and a garage.
- *Gripley Grip*: the rough handle tape, "for people who mean it."

## 4. The Resurfacer Saga

### Zambelli and the Model 525

Frank Zambelli ran a refrigeration business and a small outdoor rink in the
lake town of Port Lucerne. Clearing the ice between skating sessions took
three men, a tractor, a barrel of hot water and an hour. In 1949, in the
shed behind his rink, he welded a scraper, a snow tank and a towel onto a
war-surplus tractor chassis, and the Zambelli Model A cleaned the whole
sheet in ten minutes with one driver. By 1955 every rink in the GNHL wanted
one.

The machine everyone pictures is the **Zambelli Model 525**, built from 1965
to 1989 and still running in hundreds of community rinks, including the
Millbrook Community Arena. It is a big boxy gas-powered machine about 1.9 m
wide, 3.6 m long and 2 m tall: a tall cream-painted snow tank with rounded top
edges filling the front two thirds, a blue band and pinstripe around it, a
chrome Z on the front badge, two round headlamps, and a squat engine cowl at
the back beside the driver's perch, where the operator sits high on the left,
in a parka, steering with the calm of someone who has done it nine thousand
times. Behind it trails the conditioner and the towel. It has a top speed of
about 14 km/h, an exhaust stack that coughs once when it starts, a
four-cylinder engine that runs on gasoline and stubbornness, and a smell of
warm oil and wet wool that every hockey player can recognise with their eyes
shut. The Millbrook rink's 525, a 1974 model, is called **Doris**. Nobody
remembers why.

### "It is not a zambelli. It is a Zambelli brand ice resurfacer."

The Model 525 was so successful that its name became the word. Everyone calls
every ice resurfacer a "zambelli," in the same way everyone calls every
tissue whatever they call it. Zambelli Inc. hates this with a passion that
has outlived its founder. Its legal department has written to dictionaries,
broadcasters, children's authors and, in 1982, a primary school in Kessler
whose winter play was called *The Little Zambelli That Could*. The school
changed the title to *The Little Zambelli Brand Ice Resurfacer That Could*,
which ran for two hours longer and was a great success. Saturday Night Hockey
commentators are contractually required to say "the Zambelli" with a capital
letter in their voice.

### The rivals

**Flatley Ice Machines (founded 1952).** Walt Flatley was Frank Zambelli's
business partner, welder and brother-in-law: they married sisters, June and
Rose Petrakis. In 1951 the partners had a disagreement over who had thought
of dragging a wet towel behind the machine to lay down the final film of
water. Walt said it was his idea. Frank said it was his, and that Walt had
merely "supplied a towel." Walt left, took the towel (the actual towel, now
in a museum), and started Flatley Ice Machines in the next town over.

What followed was the longest patent dispute in the history of the region.
The Wet Towel Patent was filed by both companies within the same week in
1953, granted to Zambelli in 1956, appealed by Flatley in 1957, overturned in
1961, reinstated in 1966, and finally settled in 1984, by which time the
patent had expired and both founders were in their seventies. The settlement
says Flatley may use a towel, Zambelli may use a towel, and neither company
may describe itself in advertising as "the original." Both now advertise
themselves as "the first."

For thirty-three years the two families had Christmas dinner together,
because June and Rose would not hear otherwise. Frank and Walt sat at
opposite ends of the table and spoke only through their wives. ("June, would
you ask your sister's husband to pass the gravy." "Rose, tell your sister's
husband the gravy was my recipe.")

Flatley's great product was the **Flatliner F-9** (1971), a battery-electric
resurfacer twenty years ahead of its time and roughly twenty minutes short
of a full charge. At the 1972 Final in Kingsport, the F-9 stopped dead at
centre ice halfway through the second intermission, and it took forty
minutes, two tow ropes and a team of Bramford defencemen to push it off. The
Intermission That Lasted Forty Minutes set Flatley back a generation. Its
machines today are quiet, clean, electric, a little smaller than a Zambelli
and painted Flatley green, and they work fine. Nobody calls them "a
flatley."

**Polarix Ice Systems (founded 2009).** A modern firm that makes a
self-driving electric resurfacer, the **Polarix Glide**, which maps the ice
with lasers and needs no driver. It is very good. It is also very literal:
at the 2015 Sunvale home opener, a Glide refused to leave the ice because
the Scorpions mascot was standing on its route, and the two circled each
other for six minutes while the crowd chanted for the mascot. Polarix
insists its products are not resurfacers but "ice preparation platforms,"
partly for marketing and partly so Zambelli can't send them letters.

### The Kingsport Parade Incident (1966)

When the Monarchs won the 1966 Cup, the city invited both Zambelli and
Flatley to drive a machine in the victory parade, on the assumption that
they were grown men. Frank Zambelli drove the brand-new Model 525 himself.
Walt Flatley drove a Flatley Model 7. They were supposed to be six floats
apart. On Harbour Street, Walt overtook the brass band. Frank overtook the
Mayor. For six blocks the two founders raced each other through downtown
Kingsport at fourteen kilometres per hour, side by side, neither able to pull
ahead, waving to the crowd and resurfacing the road. The race ended when
both machines mounted the kerb at the same moment and came to rest in the
fountain in Victoria Square. The Puckworth Cup, which was riding on the
Monarchs' float behind them, was briefly in the fountain too.

The photo of the two men standing knee-deep in the fountain, not looking at
each other, hangs in both companies' head offices, each cropped to show only
the other man looking guilty.

### Where they are now

Zambelli Inc. is still family owned, still makes gas, propane and electric
machines, still sells refurbished 525s to rinks that love them, and still
writes letters. Flatley is owned by a holding company and makes good electric
machines that nobody gets emotional about. Polarix is backed by investors and
is "disrupting ice." All three show up at the Northern Rink Operators' trade
show every spring with booths positioned, at Zambelli's insistence, as far
apart as the hall allows.

## 6. How the Lore Shows Up in the Game

- **Rink banners.** The old Millbrook Community Arena has faded sponsor
  banners from the seventies (Maple Tire, Moosebrew, Big Gord's earliest ad
  with more hair, Grizzly Gas) that get replaced with modern ones as you
  renovate. Bigger towns carry more and newer sponsors; Duncannon has a
  Frostbank centre-ice logo and a TeleNorth video board.
- **Board ads by tier.** Beer League rinks carry local sponsors (Big Gord's,
  Maple Tire, Grizzly Gas, Puckhead Pizza). Senior AAA and up add Moosebrew,
  Brokenbone and Double-Double; Minor League Pro adds Frostbank, TeleNorth,
  Rink Rage and Northwind Air.
- **Pro shop stock.** The pro shop sells by tier and budget: Honest Hockey and
  Shorewood in Millbrook, CMC and Kodiak in Harlow Falls and Kessler, Warlock
  and Ironside from Port Ainsley up, and Grafton only in Duncannon, by
  appointment. Gear stats can lean on brand personality (Grafton: best edge,
  worst price; Honest: average everything, cheapest; Shorewood: great feel,
  breaks sooner; Warlock: hardest shot, flashy).
- **The resurfacer.** Millbrook's machine is Doris, a 1974 Zambelli Model 525,
  cream with the blue band. Renovating can restore her in club colours or
  swap her for a Flatley electric; a Polarix Glide is a late-game luxury
  that occasionally refuses to move.
- **Broadcast trivia.** Loading screens, intermission cards and the radio in
  the car on the region map can play "Did you know?" lines from Saturday Night
  Hockey and Moose on the Loose: the Blizzard Charter, the Wrong Twin, the
  Parade Incident, the Toe Goal, the Sandwich Clause.
- **Legends' jerseys in Millbrook.** Ronnie Haverstock's number 14 hangs over
  the Millbrook ice, beside his milk-uniform photo. The lobby has the 2005
  photo of the Millbrook Mallards with the Puckworth Cup, and a glass case
  with a Shorewood stick, a frozen Double-Double cup and Doris's original
  keys. Renovating reframes and relights them, never removes them.
- **The Millbrook bank.** The dead spot in the boards behind the Millbrook
  net stays in the rink as a playable quirk: the puck dies there instead of
  bouncing. Haverstock taught the valley to use it. Fixing it during
  renovation is possible and the town will be upset.
- **Ronnie in person.** Haverstock plays in the Thursday old-timers' game and
  can appear as a mentor, a scrimmage opponent or the man who hands you the
  Duncannon Dukes' phone number.
- **The phone call.** At Minor League Pro, the red rotary phone in the Dukes'
  GM office is the final goal of the career: one call from Kingsport and
  you're in the show.
- **Names in the world.** Team names, sponsor names and brand names in this
  file are the canon for jerseys, signs, the pro shop and broadcast text, and
  `canon.json` holds them as data with colours for pixel-art use.
